The previous writeup "za - next" laid down some useful ideas,
but it got big and wooly. Here I try to recollect around the
idea of *growth* of:

*   Settlement sizes
*   Leadership roles
*   Economic exchange
*   Infrastructure
*   Ritual complexity

# Initial Discussion

I have posited that in villages with low surplus, conflict
grows with population size, traditional relationships become
harder to maintain, and eventually people break up. Keep in
mind that low surplus is a standard state, as people will
often "satisfice". However, if crops come at only a few times
in the year, then people will tend to over-save for safety,
resulting in some amount of surplus, though it might not be
that big.

Surplus doesn't automatically cause growth, because people
could just throw it away, but in general, they'd be interested
in more useful uses:

*   Use spare time from surplus on handicrafts, infrastructure,
    gossiping, etc.
*   Trade surplus food for better and more varied foods, or
    use in recipes that taste better but require more inputs
*   Trade surplus food locally for labor to farm more, build
    more, etc.
*   Trade food at longer distances for other articles
*   Give away to get goodwill

If people are just satisficing, they could just take more
leisure time and give more away for goodwill. And in fact
many people seem to do roughly that. I now posit that there
are some "personality" differences that make some people more
interested in certain roles.

One of the best documented such roles is apparently the
"big man", a sort of general social entrepreneur who manages
wealth, labor, loyalties, etc. However, the "big man" per se
is perhaps a feature of certain societies, not found in quite
the same way in all. In particular, looking ahead, it seems
that our setting will develop religious-flavored leadership,
so perhaps leadership had a religious and corporate character
relatively early on.

It's also helpful to review the needs and desires that people
have that can be addressed by our model:

*   Peace - a state of reasonably cooperative stable relations
    with neighbors and powerful beings
*   Marriage
*   Food security - basic needs reliably met
*   Luxuries - even early on, it can be safely assumed that
    there are certain things, such as special foods and
    festivals, that people particularly want
*   Status - some will want more than others

Another point is that economies and other benefits of scale
are one of the key drivers of "growth". So, a natural question
is, for each of the needs, what were early economies of scale,
and how did they work?

*   Food production
    *   Extra labor to build irrigated farms
    *   Managers of construction/maintenance can be like
        "owners" of the irrigation works, but at the beginning
        it would be understood they should manage for general
        benefit
    *   Extra labor to build bigger fishing boats and nets
*   Food security
    *   Bigger granary can be better designed, to hold food more
        safely with easier disbursement
    *   Leaders can coordinate transfers, possibly faster and
        better than distributed versions
*   Luxuries
    *   May have to be acquired by trade missions or long collection
        journeys
    *   Could also apply to key trade goods such as flint
*   Marriage
    *   Clans will compete to be better marriage partners
*   Peace
    *   Useful to have allies when dealing with threats
    *   Like other relationships, gets hard to have everyone
        connected to everyone with large N, may start to see
        clusters and hierarchies
    *   When not everyone knows everyone, presumably people need
        to dress or otherwise be identifiable as belonging
        => need for new goods and styles

Now, what about settlement size growth? We know that at first,
villages tend to fission, and we think it's mainly because of
local conflicts, not resource scarcity. So, a village could 
grow if local conflicts are reduced *or* there is some benefit
to living in the larger settlement. At small scales, conflict
is quadratic, so it seems we'd probably need new ways of keeping
the peace to grow much at first. At larger scales, conflict
scales more like N**(7/6), so adding amenities could generally
balance out the conflict.

Some of the first sites to grow seem to have been religious in
character. Let's posit this:

*   Relatively early on, cereal farming becomes highly productive,
    so that "thanks to" cereals, people are better fed and have
    better food secuity => happiness.
*   But there are also destructive floods => fear.
*   People attribute the bounty or loss to powerful beings, and believe
    that they have to reciprocate to them to keep the bounty
    coming => regular sacrifice rituals, which are important to
    people's psychological safety, and notionally have a good
    or bad effect on output.
    *   People will attribute some output differences to skill
        and effort at farming, but hidden factors such as subtle
        traits, secret knowledge, and/or environment are more
        likely to be attributed to divine favor
    *   Each clan does their own fertility rites, thus clans
        with more (agricultural and birth) fertility can be
        inferred to have more divine favor
    *   There are also village-level rites, which there should
        probably be at least for floods, since those affect all,
        and probably for fertility as well, as river and weather
        conditions will affect things
*   Now, if a village has higher output than others OR more impressive
    rituals, it can be seen as being more favored.
    *   This effect is compounded if the village has BOTH better
        rituals and better results
    *   This will make the village more appealing and allows growth,
        but note we haven't done anything about conflict yet.
    *   But something with religious credibility might be able to
        settle conflicts more readily. Perhaps either party to a
        conflict can appeal to a religious authority, who then can
        reduce conflict costs and gain credibility.

# Growth Factors

Back to things that should grow:

*   Settlement sizes
*   Leadership roles
*   Economic exchange
*   Infrastructure
*   Ritual complexity

For a settlement to grow we need conflict reduction and then
other benefits to living in a town, such as better food security,
better products and services, more religious access, better
farming infrastructure. The other benefits would actually be
able to provide some growth without conflict reduction, but
probably not a lot, as conflict can scale up rapidly.

Conflict reduction could happen in multiple ways:

*   Ritual or other leaders gain more influence and can resolve
    disputes.
*   People reorient from unstable equal relationships to stabler
    unequal relationships. (cf. accessing consumer products in 
    the modern day)
*   New ideologies promoting conflict reduction (probably a
    meaningful but relatively weak factor on its own, but stronger
    in conjunction with others)

The key appears to be the appearance of some kind of power
inequality (economic, ritual, and political) that allows for
some centralization of peacekeeping. Economic is highly relevant
because they'll need a resource base to perform in other fields,
though we note that ritual success or political influence will
also help build wealth. Economic means they can also give gifts
and support directly to gain influence. Ritual power gives
another way to reward and punish, and also general prominence,
"specialness", and promoting ideologies of power; it generally
takes some wealth and popularity to pull off a great ritual.

On the amenities side, in today's model we can readily fit in:

*   Better land or other special endowments - the natural locale
    might itself be an amenity.
*   Cheaper/better irrigation infrastructure - economies of scale
    allow building infrastructure more cheaply and with more
    sophisticated controls, boosting farm output
*   Cheaper/better/more secure food storage

But we need more, as ritual is one of our key early boosts:

*   Bigger and better rituals
    *   More people
    *   More variety of foods, goods, and performances
    *   Economies of scale
*   More ritual infrastructure
    *   Economies of scale
*   More of a market for special goods and services
    *   More producers can collect
    *   More buyers
    *   Economies of scale on trade missions

The big three for the start state appear to be rituals,
irrigation, and food security. Thus, we should be able to add
some structure around those and see growth.

## Food Security and Storage

At first, people rely on own stores and on neighbors for food
security. We could imagine them deciding to build a bigger and
better collective granary, but it's far from clear why they
would do that.

However, clans seeking influence might try to build up more
storage, and then they would have reason to invest in better
storage systems. We can think of them as somewhat running a
bank.

## Irrigation

At first, we can have people farming natural stands of alluvium,
but this should probably have pretty variable yields due to
random flooding and river shifts, as well as relatively low amount
of land available.

Soon, they'll learn to protect those areas with ditches, raising
yields and making them more consistent.

Then they'll learn to construct new flooded fields, starting in
ways that don't require much coordination. So local populations
could grow from that...if conflict can be reduced. It should also
be set so that this option opens up more settlement sites, so
at first it might mainly enable more villages.

Eventually there will be an option to create increasingly large
canals to build new farms, and this will require coordination.
Leaders and workers are both needed, and both are interdependent.
This opens up more settlement sites as well as local growth
opportunities.

## Rituals

We need rituals both to keep the peace and provide amenities. Also,
ritual is probably intertwined with various other things. Here's an
attempt to lay something out:

Let's imagine that from the start, people are giving thanks for the
waters and the harvests, separately and collectively as a settlement.
Any growth and success that they have will boost the credibility of
associated rituals. This can play out in a few different ways:

*   Clan rituals might gain credibility so that others want to
    participate in some way. The clan already "owns" their own
    rituals, so others will be expected to provide gifts for this.
    Eventually, a clan might be providing ritual services to many
    other clans, even beyond the settlement.
*   For settlement-level rituals, we imagine that there is a variety
    of roles, according to the ritual structure, that different clans
    can take on. People will want a good ritual, so there will be
    pressure to get each clan doing what it's best at, which could
    be leading the ritual in some cases.
*   The first "temples" may have been primarily places to receive
    offerings, and so they might have been basically for clan rituals
    offered to others. They add impressiveness, but also permanence:
    it's now a strong ritual resource that a clan could control for
    a long time - or that could rotate among clans.
    *   Later, they got bigger and more plausibly could have hosted
        settlement-level rituals. Maybe there was a sequence of
        increasingly large scopes, first 1-1, then 1-some (elders?),
        then 1-many (public rituals).
    *   That sequence also implies an increasing ability to "capture"
        more of the ritual needs and key interactions in a special
        building.
*   Settlement-level rituals and buildings give people something
    new in common, implying a closer sense of relatedness to others
    in the settlement, as well as the possibility of gradations of
    relatedness with the nearby villages that also sometimes go to
    the town.
*   Ritual leaders will particularly gain influence that helps them
    resolve disputes.
*   Note that it takes wealth to build a temple, but once you've got
    it, you can collect more donations and possibly gain even more
    wealth. That in turn would give more power that can also be used
    to moderate disputes and build more infrastructure.

# Personality and Strategy Factors

Not everyone wants to go to the trouble to do the work to gain status
and lead major projects. We'll have a personality trait that's something
like "ambition", but could just as well be called "piety" as in our
setting it's not clear it was as self-aggrandizing as in some others.

We can also think about big 5 traits, which probably do apply.

*   Conscientiousness: Works hard and plays by the rules, which are
    separable traits, with hard work apparently more important for
    income. "Wants to produce more of a surplus" will be a key factor
    here, but note that this trait doesn't imply much about what they
    do with that surplus.
*   Extraversion: Wants external rewards. Clans with this trait will
    want to use any surplus that they have to build relationships,
    and also buy popular stuff and other related rewards of the more
    social and external type. Clans low in this trait may self-invest
    their surpluses instead.
*   Agreeableness: We'll take this to be a generalized tendency to
    seek cooperation or exploitation. It would influence decisions
    like playing hawk.
*   Emotional Stability: This one seems to be about sensitivity to
    pains and punishments. In general, it's a pretty murky trait,
    but it may have something to do with risk aversion and sensitivity
    to punishment.
*   Intellect/Imagination: This apparently isn't necessarily recognized
    as a trait by different peoples, but they do recognize skill differences,
    including in oratory.

Note that people can act more conscientious or agreeable somewhat as
demanded, and to a lesser extent for other traits. But what we're looking
for is traits and customs that can be durable across generations (though
they don't have to be). 

*   "Ambition"/"Piety"/"General Factor" - tendency to work hard to
    collect resources and then mobilize them for social rewards.
*   Intellect/Imagination - ability to learn and invent; relevant
    to coordination and oratory for leadership

# Item List

The rough sequence might be:

*   Hunting and gathering, including gathering of cereals
*   Hunting and gathering with some cultivation
*   Small surpluses and minor status differences appear
*   Ditching for better yields, early collective project
    that can be aided by level 1 leadership ("pious ones")
*   Simple canal irrigation allowing more villages to be
    founded
*   Few villages
*   First "temples" possibly providing level 2 leadership
*   Several villages
*   Temple sites producing more amenities

We'll need to firm up the model for each step.

*   Local food exchange with status rise for givers
    *   Need "piety" trait to influence how much they want to
        grow and give
*   Clan self rituals and authority
*   Clan ritual provision to other clans
*   Level 1 (hortator) village leader possible, giving possibility
    of dispute resolution
*   Level 1 (hortator) ditching leader possible, giving bonuses
    to construction speed and quality and reducing scale losses
    *   Need "brains" trait to influence quality of results
*   Level 1 (hortator) ritual leader possible, giving possibility
    of dispute resolution and bonuses to ritual success
    *   Need settlement-level rituals for this
*   Canal irrigation and founding of more/better villages,
    also possible expansion of existing villages
*   Level 1 (offering shrine) temple possible, giving boost to
    ritual authority and dispute resolution

At this point, we have ways to reduce conflict, increase local
population supportable by farmland, and boost productivity
(and thus per capita incomes, and thus appeal), which should
be enough for settlements to grow some. The temple will also
add to amenities if credibility is good. Finally, with the 
offering shrines, we may have some clan receiving enough donations
to start building a more advanced granary as a further amenity.
